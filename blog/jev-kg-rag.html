Jev and KG-RAG: Comparing Two Scoring Approaches

Revisiting KG-RAG’s progress, proximity, and BFS search after learning about Jev.

After learning about Jev, I started revisiting a component of our EMNLP 2025 paper, KG-RAG. We used an LLM to score candidate GUI trajectories, then let a search algorithm decide which paths to explore. Jev also exposes bounded probabilistic judgments that ordinary code can act on.

The resemblance is meaningful. It is strongest at the boundary between a model’s judgment and a program’s control flow. Looking more closely, however, reveals differences in what is being searched, how scores are computed, and what training is intended to make reliable.

I wanted to use one example to understand when the scoring rules make the same choice, when they disagree, and what Jev might suggest for a future version of our trajectory scorer.

1 The shared idea: a model as a judge inside a program

KG-RAG addresses missing app-specific navigation knowledge. Its offline pipeline explores a UI Transition Graph (UTG), uses intent milestones to evaluate trajectories, and stores useful intent–trajectory pairs for online retrieval. Candidate paths are expanded in batches, scored, and filtered to a top-K frontier. The search is over UI paths; it is not a token-decoding tree. [Paper §3.2 and Algorithm 3]

For example, imagine two milestones: reach the settings screen, then open the privacy policy. We can ask how many milestones a trajectory has completed: zero, one, or two. Extracting the relevant LLM logits and applying softmax gives a distribution over those answers.

This is the useful connection to structured scoring: the model supplies numbers over an explicit answer space, and the surrounding program decides what those numbers mean for the task.

Jev makes that interface explicit through question types such as Noul for yes/no probability, Choice for categories, and Score for ordered levels. Choice and Score also return a separate confidence statistic. For our milestone example, the closest match is Score. [TypeSafe primitives]

2 One example, four different questions

Suppose the distribution for a candidate trajectory is:

Our progress score: how large is the strongest probability?

Progress=max⁡(0.1, 0.2, 0.7)=0.7\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Progress} &= \max(0.1,\,0.2,\,0.7) \\ &= 0.7\end{aligned}Progress​=max(0.1,0.2,0.7)=0.7​

There are two different numbers here: “2” is the model’s most likely completion count; “0.7” is the probability it assigns to that answer. The paper’s progress definition uses the latter. So 0.7 does not mean “70% of the task is done.” Keeping only the largest probability also loses the rest of the distribution—which is where the next score can help.

Our proximity score: does the ranking favor more progress?

Start with an intuitive question: does the model lean toward more rather than less completion? The ordering we want to favor is “two completed” first, “one completed” second, and “none completed” last: [2, 1, 0]. Our example has 70% > 20% > 10%, so it matches. Algorithm 2 compares the actual ordering with that target, squares the difference at each position, and puts a minus sign in front:

Proximity=−[(2−2)2+(1−1)2+(0−0)2]=0\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Proximity} &= -\bigl[(2-2)^2 + (1-1)^2 \\ &\qquad + (0-0)^2\bigr] \\ &= 0\end{aligned}Proximity​=−[(2−2)2+(1−1)2+(0−0)2]=0​

Same progress score, different proximity

Now compare two candidates. Both give “two milestones completed” a 70% probability, so their progress scores are tied:

Look at the remaining 30%. For A, the model’s next-best guess is “one milestone completed.” For B, it is “none completed.” Put another way: if the model is wrong about finishing both, A has the more promising fallback. That is useful information the shared progress score of 0.7 leaves out.

A ranks the outcomes [2, 1, 0], exactly matching the target ordering. B ranks them [2, 0, 1]: the last two positions are swapped. B therefore gets a penalty of −(0² + 1² + 1²) = −2, while A gets 0. With progress tied, KG-RAG prefers A.

Why prefer zero? It means zero ordering penalty, not zero progress. Squared differences can only be zero or positive; the minus sign turns them into zero or negative scores. Zero is the highest possible score because nothing disagrees with the target ordering. Think of starting at zero and deducting points for misplaced outcomes.

This also explains the name “proximity”: it measures closeness to an ideal ordering, not the number of UI steps left. A score of zero does not prove that the task is finished. For example, [0.32, 0.33, 0.35] has the same ordering as A and also gets zero, despite being much less decisive. Proximity uses ranks; it discards the sizes of the probability gaps. [Paper Algorithm 2]

The trajectory evaluator still has to judge whether the actions actually completed the milestones. Proximity only summarizes the ordering of that evaluator’s probabilities.

Jev’s Score: how much progress is expected?

Score=0×0.1+1×0.2+2×0.7=1.6\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Score} &= 0\times0.1 + 1\times0.2 \\ &\qquad + 2\times0.7 \\ &= 1.6\end{aligned}Score​=0×0.1+1×0.2+2×0.7=1.6​

This is the probability-weighted mean of the level numbers. It averages milestone counts, not logits. “1.6” is an expectation under the distribution; it does not mean the interface literally completed a fractional milestone. [Official Score documentation]

Jev’s Score confidence: how concentrated is the prediction?

The most likely level is two. The probability-weighted distance from it is 0.1 × 2 + 0.2 × 1 = 0.4. Jev normalizes that spread by a uniform-distribution baseline—2/3 for three levels—and subtracts it from one, flooring the result at zero:

Confidence=max⁡(0, 1−0.423)=0.4\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Confidence} &= \max\left(0,\,1-\frac{0.4}{\frac{2}{3}}\right) \\ &= 0.4\end{aligned}Confidence​=max(0,1−32​0.4​)=0.4​

This is a concentration statistic, not a 40% probability that the answer is correct. Jev’s Choice uses a different confidence formula; this post uses Score because our milestones are ordered. [Official confidence formulas]

3 Can both scoring rules find the same path?

Yes. Even in the A/B example above, both prefer A: KG-RAG breaks the 0.7 tie using proximity, while Jev’s expected score is 1.6 for A and 1.5 for B. Different arithmetic can produce the same decision.

Let’s follow a whole search. The task is still to reach Settings and open the Privacy Policy. Use a tiny top-K search with K = 1: expand one UI action per round and keep one candidate to continue. This makes each selection visible; it is a pruned search, rather than exhaustive BFS.

We give both scoring rules the same hypothetical probabilities and the same final screen check. The second version uses Jev-style expected milestone scores inside our search loop. Jev supplies judgments; the search logic still lives in our code.

Jev-style scoring also keeps Settings: its expected completion is 0.70 + 2 × 0.10 = 0.90, versus 0.20 + 2 × 0.10 = 0.40 for Help. Intuitively, one candidate looks likely to have reached the first milestone; the other looks likely to have reached neither.

The useful lesson is that different scores need not mean different behavior. What matters for pruning is which candidate ranks higher. Here, the two rules agree at every selection. This is a worked example of agreement, not evidence that two trained models always produce the same probabilities or search results. The next example shows why that distinction matters.

4 What would make more sense for our BFS?

Now compare A with a path the evaluator is very sure has made little progress:

The same distributions, summarized by four different metrics.
PathDistributionProgressProximityExpected countConfidence
A[0.1, 0.2, 0.7]0.701.60.4
C[0.9, 0.08, 0.02]0.9−80.120.82

C has higher confidence, but A is more promising. Being certain that a path fails does not make it worth expanding. If maximum probability is used as the primary ranking key, and proximity only breaks ties, C can outrank A. This is a consequence of that literal scoring rule, not a claim that we have reproduced a failure in the released implementation.

For a future ablation, I would separate three decisions:

  • What to expand: try expected milestone count as the primary progress heuristic.
  • What looks complete: inspect the probability of completing all milestones, with a task-appropriate success check.
  • What needs more attention: use uncertainty to decide whether to rescore or keep a wider frontier. Automatically pruning uncertain paths may discard good options.

Expected progress is still a heuristic, not a guarantee of eventual success. A path with fewer completed milestones may have a better continuation. Equal weighting also treats each milestone as equally valuable. The meaningful experiment is to hold the evaluator, candidate graph, and search budget fixed, vary the scoring rule, and measure successful paths found and search cost.

5 Similar interface, different systems

The graph, offline knowledge construction, and online retrieval are central to KG-RAG. Jev is a decision-model interface that can be placed inside many different programs. Sharing a scoring pattern does not make the whole systems equivalent.

AspectKG-RAG scorerJev
Model useRead bounded judgments from an instruction-tuned LLM’s logits.Expose typed decisions from a model trained for that interface.
OrchestrationIteratively expand and rank graph trajectories.Return judgments for a caller-defined workflow.
Reliability targetUseful trajectory ranking within search.Calibrated decision probabilities, according to TypeSafe.

There are also two different kinds of parallelism. Our search batches candidate paths within a round. Jev’s speculative fan-out evaluates questions against the same state before code knows which answers it will need—for example, asking both a ticket’s category and its bug severity, then ignoring severity if it is not a bug. [Official fan-out pattern]

That can remove a conditional round trip. It does not remove a real dependency: if the next question needs a newly observed UI state or a newly chosen frontier, another step is still required.

Our comparison should also use the right LLM baseline. Reading selected next-token logits can avoid generating a long textual answer for that judgment. Jev’s comparison with autoregressive JSON generation therefore does not establish the same speed advantage over our scorer. A fair test would compare batched logit scoring and Jev on the same trajectories, hardware or serving conditions, and quality target.

6 What does “specially trained” mean?

TypeSafe calls its training approach Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD). Its stated aim is to produce decisions with probabilities that match observed outcomes. It also describes a new architecture and parallel sampler. These are the company’s public descriptions, not architectural details inferred from its API. [Launch article]

Calibration is easy to picture using our example. Collect 100 trajectories for which a model assigns roughly 70% probability to full completion. If approximately 70 really are complete, that group is consistent with calibration. It is a property across predictions, not a guarantee for a particular trajectory. [Official AI primer]

Softmax alone does not establish that property. It turns logits into numbers that sum to one; it does not prove that “70%” matches a 70% success frequency. Similarly, copying Jev’s Score and confidence formulas changes how we summarize a distribution, not how reliable the underlying probabilities are.

The official materials reviewed for this post do not provide a reproducible RLCD recipe specifying the reward, training data construction, and optimization procedure. I cannot responsibly fill those gaps by claiming it uses PPO, GRPO, or a particular scoring-rule loss. The published goal is clear; the exact training mechanism remains unspecified in those sources.

For our own work, the useful next step is concrete: label milestone completion on held-out trajectories, examine calibration as well as ranking quality, and test whether improved probabilities actually improve search. A sharper distribution is not sufficient evidence of a better evaluator.

What I take from Jev is a reason to make our evaluator’s contract more explicit: how much progress it predicts, how uncertain that prediction is, and how each signal should change the search.
